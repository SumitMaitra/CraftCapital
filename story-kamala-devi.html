Kamala Devi: From Riverbank Silt to Modern Architectural Spaces

How a master sculptress united 28 women artisans in Nadia district to turn an ancient folk doll tradition into a thriving B2B social enterprise.

Kamala Devi (Interviewed by Subhashree Mukherjee) Lead Artisan, Mrittika Mahila SHG Cooperative · Published August 2026

In the quiet lanes of Ghurni, the rhythmic hum of potter’s wheels has provided the soundtrack to life for over four centuries. But when Kamala Devi began sculpting at age fourteen alongside her grandfather, the community's master craftsmen were almost exclusively men.

"Girls were permitted to paint the clay or package the finished pieces," Kamala recalls with a gentle smile. "But throwing clay on the heavy wheel and formulating kiln mixtures was reserved for elders. I spent hours before sunrise practicing secretly on leftover river clay."

The Science of Jalangi River Silt

The secret behind Krishnanagar’s world-renowned realism lies in the unique alluvial silt deposited by the meandering Jalangi river. Unlike coarse mountain clay or sticky red mud, Jalangi silt possesses an ultra-fine grain size that allows artisans to pinch facial details as delicate as eyelashes and the flowing drapes of a Bengali cotton saree.

Founding the Women's Pottery Collective

In 2018, faced with declining tourist footfall and middlemen who paid pennies per figurine while selling them for hundreds of rupees in city showrooms, Kamala organized 28 women in her village to form the Mrittika Mahila Self-Help Group.

"When an artisan receives fair value directly from institutional buyers, it does not just buy food — it sends daughters to college and invests in safer, smoke-free electric kilns."

— Kamala Devi, State Craft Awardee

Today, Kamala's cooperative produces not only heritage miniature figures, but also modern minimalist unglazed planters, terracotta tableware, and bespoke hospitality installations for five-star eco-resorts across India.
